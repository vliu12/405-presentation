Why this matters now: drones are moving toward civilians
Drone attacks in conflict rose about 4,000% from 2020 to 2024, and civilians are increasingly the target. Five shifts have turned drone fear from a niche counterterrorism issue into a mass civilian experience, in war zones and in countries that are not at war.
2.1 Scale: drones are now everywhere in war
• CIVIC's analysis of ACLED data estimates drone attacks in conflict settings rose about 4,000% from 2020 to 2024, from about 4,525 in 2023 to 19,704 in 2024 (Just Security).
• ACLED's 2026 Conflict Index reports air and drone strikes at an all-time high (ACLED). ACLED also counts more than 450 armed groups that used drones in attacks over five years (Infobae).
• Ukraine made about 800,000 drones in 2023, 2.2 million in 2024 and at least 4 million in 2025. The U.S. makes about 100,000 combat drones a year (Euromaidan Press). Russia aimed for 2 million FPV drones in 2025 alone (Kyiv Independent).
2.2 Cost: cheap enough to aim at individual people
• An early-2000s U.S. military drone cost around $7 million. Commercial drones now cost as little as about $2,000 (Just Security), and some Ukrainian FPV models sell for roughly $360 (Vector).
• Why it matters: a Predator was saved for high-value targets. A $400 drone can be spent on one person on a bicycle, a car on a road or a market stall, so ordinary daily movement becomes something to fear.
2.3 Targeting: civilians are now a primary target
• Ukraine. In 2025, drone attacks on civilians outnumbered every other type of attack on civilians for the first time (ACLED). Russian drone attacks rose 150% year on year (Kyiv Independent). For several months in 2025, FPV drones were the leading cause of civilian casualties (CIVIC). Kherson's "human safari" (Section 3.5) is the extreme case.
• Sudan. ACLED recorded a 600% rise in drone-related deaths and an 81% rise in drone attacks in 2025 (AP via WTOP). Drones killed at least 880 civilians from January to April 2026, hitting hospitals, schools, markets and displacement camps (Arab News). A UN official said the pattern of targets shows intent to spread terror (Al Jazeera).
• Gaza. Surveillance drones (zanana) have flown since the early 2000s, and residents report they stayed constant after the October 2025 ceasefire: surveillance outlasting the fighting.
• Aid. Drone incidents affecting health care or aid were under 10 a year until 2022, then 84 in 2023 and 308 in 2024 (CIVIC, citing Insecurity Insight).
2.4 Spillover: drone fear in countries not at war
• Europe, autumn 2025. Unidentified drones closed Copenhagen airport for nearly four hours on September 22 and shut Oslo, Munich (twice), Brussels and Liège (International Airport Review). Denmark called it a hybrid attack and banned civilian drone flights September 29 to October 3 (The Debrief). Analysts argue such flights may be designed to provoke anxiety and erode trust in government protection.
• New Jersey, late 2024. Night-time sightings caused panic across the U.S. Northeast. The FBI and other agencies received over 5,000 tips, and the FAA imposed temporary drone bans (USA Today via AOL). The White House later said most drones were FAA-authorized, but many residents stayed unconvinced.
Why this is useful evidence: New Jersey is a near-natural experiment for step 1 of our mechanism. No strike happened and no one was hurt, yet uncertainty about who was flying the drones was enough to cause widespread anxiety. Europe shows the same effect produced on purpose.
2.5 Normalization: more drones over everyday life
• U.S. police drone use has nearly tripled since 2020 (Deseret News).
• Executive Order 14307, "Unleashing American Drone Dominance" (June 6, 2025), directs the FAA to enable routine beyond-visual-line-of-sight flights under a new rule, Part 108. Drones overhead will become ordinary.
• The same cheap quadcopters used for deliveries, photography and policing are the platforms modified for war. A drone overhead no longer signals whether it means harm.
2.6 Synthesis: why the burden is shifting to civilians
Our interpretation of the evidence above, not a claim from any single source:

1.  Cheapness makes it affordable to aim drones at individuals and everyday places.
2.  Persistence keeps loitering and surveillance drones overhead for hours, creating the constant-threat condition.
3.  Deniability lets drones fly covertly over countries not at war, which is hard to attribute or answer.
4.  Dual use makes military and civilian drones look and sound alike.
5.  Fear as a goal. In Kherson, Sudan and Europe, investigators and officials describe psychological pressure as an aim, not a side effect.
    For the presentation: this answers "why now?" and supports the future-of-warfare framing. The trend points toward more and cheaper drones, more civilian targeting, and more drones in peacetime skies.
